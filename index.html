    The Method

    Okay, but how is it in the know?

    I turned Austin into a graph, gave every place its own rules, and taught the planner how a girl actually spends a Saturday. It keeps track of the time, the neighborhood, the drive, when you last ate, the weather and how much energy you have left. It knows what belongs before brunch and what never happens right after Pilates. Then it finds the one day that actually fits. On Saturdays, we brunch.

    Austin is a weighted graph
    Neighborhoods are nodes and roads are edges weighted in drive minutes. Dijkstra’s algorithm with a min-heap finds the fastest drive between any two, and every answer is cached. With No car, each place gets its real coordinates from OpenStreetMap, walks are measured with the haversine formula, and a breadth-first search keeps only places you can reach in hops of a mile or less.
    A weighted lottery picks the candidates
    Each run draws a shortlist with weighted sampling without replacement (Efraimidis–Spirakis: every place gets the key random()1/weight and the biggest keys win). Higher-rated places and closer places get more tickets, and on a Saturday morning brunch always gets one.
    Dynamic programming finds the best day
    Bitmask dynamic programming over subsets: a state is which places you’ve been to, the last one, when you finish it, and a tiny “person state” (just worked out, just ate, just changed). Each move has to be allowed by the rules, and the day that wins scores 10 points per point of joy minus travel, off-hours timing and a little energy per stop. That turns billions of possible orders into a search that runs in a fraction of a second, in your browser.
    The rules are data, not if-statements
    Every kind of stop carries metadata in rules.py: how filling it is, whether it’s a workout, whether you can show up in workout clothes, when it closes, the hours it’s best at. One function, step(), decides if a move is something a person would do. That’s also where the little “why?” notes come from.
    Every Saturday has a number
    The randomness is seeded, so Saturday #184 is always the same Saturday.
    And it’s tested
    A backtracking search that really tries every possible order runs on dozens of random days, and the dynamic programming has to match its best score every time. Other tests check the people rules one by one: Pilates, then coffee, then a shower is fine; Pilates, then shopping is not; brunch and then lunch 45 minutes later never happens; nothing is ever scheduled outside its hours.

    Editor’s letter

    Dear reader,

    I gave Python my favorite Austin spots and, naturally, my opinions. Lattes are for mornings. Saturday brunch comes with mimosas, obviously. After Pilates, one showers before being seen in public. And I refuse to send you from South Congress to the Domain and back; a girl has standards. Tell me your hours, your mood and your energy. I’ll handle the rest.

    Every Saturday this planner makes is one I would actually spend. The spots are the ones I keep going back to. The rules are the ones I live by. The algorithm is the part where I made Python agree with me.
